The [Vaultwarden update post](/blog/updating-vaultwarden-with-codex-and-verified-backups) needed one image near the top. The idea seemed simple: put my old process on the left and the Codex process on the right.

My old process was two steps: redeploy the container, then hope the vault came back. Codex did something much more deliberate. It found the running version, chose a compatible target, checked the existing backup, made a clean backup, copied it off the server, changed the container, and verified the result.

The first diagram contained all of that. It also proved that having the right boxes is not the same as explaining the process.

## Version 1: the idea was clear, but the route was not

![The first diagram, with a short old process beside eight Codex cards arranged without phase bands.](/images/posts/improving-a-diagram-with-a-review-skill/version-1.svg)

*Version 1 had the right contrast and the wrong reading burden.*

The left side worked immediately. It was intentionally sparse and a little uncomfortable. The right side had eight tidy cards with arrows between them. Each card made sense on its own.

The trouble was the sequence. A reader could scan down the columns, move across the rows, or treat the cards as separate checks. The diagram knew the order. The reader had to infer it.

I ran the image and its intended message through a diagram-review skill. The first review scored it at 8.2 out of 10. More useful than the score was the diagnosis: the high-level contrast was clear, but the right side had stronger card pairings than a step-by-step path.

The skill recommended four named phases:

1. Inspect
2. Prove rollback
3. Change
4. Verify

That was the structural fix. The diagram did not need more decoration. It needed a route.

## Version 2: name the phases

![The second diagram, with the Codex steps grouped into four labeled horizontal phase bands.](/images/posts/improving-a-diagram-with-a-review-skill/version-2.svg)

*Version 2 made the process teachable by grouping the eight checks into four phases.*

The phase bands changed the diagram from a list into an argument. Codex did not perform eight equally weighted tasks. It established the current state, proved the rollback path, made the change, and checked the result.

I also made the first mutation explicit. Nothing touched the running service until step 6. That small line carried a lot of the trust story.

The second review scored the revision at 9.1. The main structure now worked. The remaining issues were smaller and more concrete: the handoff between phases was easy to miss, several support lines were too dense, and the final success mark looked attached to a card instead of the whole process.

That is where the review skill became especially useful. A second pass did not repeat the first critique. It found the next layer of friction.

## Version 3: make it work inside the article

![The final compact comparison diagram, with a stacked heading, four clear Codex phases, and a verified result.](/images/posts/updating-vaultwarden-with-codex-and-verified-backups/safe-update-path.svg)

*Version 3 keeps the phase structure, shortens the copy, clarifies each handoff, and fits the article.*

The last pass tightened the diagram instead of redesigning it again. I replaced ordinary handoff arrows with clear transition markers. I shortened the card copy. I moved the success state to the end of the full sequence. I also added the checks Codex intentionally skipped, because the boundary of the verification matters too.

Then I looked at the diagram in the actual blog page. The header was consuming too much of a laptop screen. It had an eyebrow, a two-column title and subtitle, and enough empty space to feel like a second article header.

I removed the eyebrow and stacked the title over the subtitle. I also found a global paragraph margin leaking into the diagram component. Fixing those two things reduced the figure to 783 pixels tall at a 1440 by 900 viewport. The header itself dropped to 110 pixels. The complete comparison now fits in one laptop view.

That final change did not come from studying the SVG by itself. It came from seeing the diagram where a reader would see it.

## What the skill actually changed

The skill reviews diagrams in a useful order: message, structure, reading path, relationships, hierarchy, legibility, consistency, and then polish. That order prevents a common failure mode where a reviewer starts adjusting colors while the reader still cannot tell what connects to what.

It also separates severity from taste. A broken reading path is a high-priority problem. A slightly weak border is not. For this diagram, that meant I could fix one structural issue in each pass:

- Version 1 needed phases.
- Version 2 needed clearer handoffs and less text.
- Version 3 needed to fit its real page.

The useful loop was simple: make the diagram, review the interpretation cost, fix the highest-priority issue, and render it again in context. I stopped when the remaining feedback no longer changed the reader's mental model.
